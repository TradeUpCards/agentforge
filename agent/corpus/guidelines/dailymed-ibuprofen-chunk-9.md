# Ibuprofen — FDA-Label Clinical Summary

## Indications and Usage

Ibuprofen is a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) indicated for the relief of signs and symptoms of rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis, the treatment of primary dysmenorrhea, the treatment of mild to moderate pain, and the reduction of fever. Prescription-strength ibuprofen formulations are indicated for these conditions at higher doses than over-the-counter versions. Ibuprofen is relevant in clinical decision support for its drug-interaction profile — it interacts with anticoagulants (warfarin), ACE inhibitors/ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics, and other NSAIDs.

## Contraindications

Ibuprofen is contraindicated in the setting of coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery — perioperative use increases the risk of myocardial infarction and stroke. It is contraindicated in patients with known hypersensitivity (including aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, manifesting as urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, or anaphylaxis) to ibuprofen, aspirin, or other NSAIDs. Ibuprofen should not be used in the third trimester of pregnancy due to the risk of premature closure of the ductus arteriosus.

## Warnings and Precautions

Ibuprofen carries a black box warning for serious cardiovascular thrombotic events — risk of myocardial infarction and stroke increases with duration of use and may occur even in patients without prior cardiovascular disease. A second black box warning covers serious gastrointestinal events including bleeding, ulceration, and perforation of the stomach or intestines, which can be fatal. Elderly patients are at highest risk for GI complications. Concomitant use with low-dose aspirin, anticoagulants (including warfarin), or corticosteroids substantially elevates GI bleeding risk. NSAIDs, including ibuprofen, can cause a dose-dependent reduction in prostaglandin synthesis in the kidney, resulting in acute renal failure, particularly in patients with pre-existing renal impairment, heart failure, liver dysfunction, or volume depletion. Ibuprofen can attenuate the antihypertensive effects of ACE inhibitors and ARBs and can increase serum potassium when used with ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or potassium-sparing diuretics — a triple combination (ACEi or ARB + NSAID + potassium-sparing diuretic or potassium supplement) carries high risk of hyperkalemia and acute kidney injury. Ibuprofen can displace warfarin from plasma protein binding and inhibit platelet function, increasing bleeding risk in anticoagulated patients.

## Dosing

For mild to moderate pain, fever, and dysmenorrhea: 200–400 mg every four to six hours, not exceeding 1,200 mg per day (OTC) or 3,200 mg per day (prescription). For arthritis: 1,200–3,200 mg per day in divided doses. Ibuprofen should be taken with food or milk to reduce GI irritation. The lowest effective dose for the shortest duration consistent with treatment goals is recommended.
